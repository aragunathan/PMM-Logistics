# LinkedIn Outbound Sequences — CEO / President / Managing Director / Owner
## Ramco Logistics Software | Outbound Prompts

> **Persona:** CEO, President, Managing Director, Owner, Founder at 3PL companies with $100M+ annual revenue
> **Objective:** Book a 15-minute executive briefing or peer introduction with Ramco's regional Managing Director
> **Rules applied:**
> - No pitch in Touch 1 — connection note establishes strategic peer relevance only
> - Business outcome language throughout — no features, no technology detail
> - Messages are shorter than CIO and COO sequences — CEOs are time-poor; every word must earn its place
> - Sender must be a senior Ramco executive — Managing Director, Country Head, or VP level. A CEO will not engage with a junior rep.
> - One CTA per message, maximum
> - Competitive framing is appropriate for this persona — they think about market position constantly
> - All messages are immediately sendable — no brackets, no placeholders
> - Regional business culture woven into tone and structure throughout
> - Read `/inputs/persona-definitions.md` CEO Persona section before using these sequences
> - All sequences align with messaging pillars in `/skills/activation/skill-03-persona-messaging-architecture.md`

# SEQUENCE 1 — AUSTRALIA
## CEO / President / Managing Director / Owner | 3PL Companies | $100M+ Revenue

---

### Touch 1 — LinkedIn Connection Request
**Character limit: 300 | Send: Day 0 | Tone: Peer executive, direct, no pitch**

> Michael — I lead Ramco's ANZ business. We work with mid-to-large 3PLs on platform infrastructure. I follow what's happening at the leading independent operators in Australia closely. Would value the connection.

---

### Touch 2 — First Message After Connection Accepted
**Send: Day 2–3 after connection | Tone: Strategic, competitive framing, no product mention**

> Thanks for connecting, Michael.
>
> I've been having a lot of conversations with 3PL leaders in Australia lately about the same underlying shift: enterprise shippers are changing what they require from a logistics partner.
>
> Real-time shipment visibility via API. Digital POD that integrates with their procurement systems. Automated invoicing with no dispute lag. A customer portal their supply chain team can log into without calling your operations team.
>
> A few years ago, only the national operators could offer this. Now it's becoming the expectation in any competitive RFP for a contract above $5 million. The 3PLs that can demonstrate this capability are winning contracts they shouldn't win on price. The ones that can't are starting to lose contracts they've held for years.
>
> I wrote a short piece on what's driving this shift and which operators are responding well. Worth 5 minutes if this is a live tension in your business.

---

### Touch 3 — The Margin Angle
**Send: Day 8–9 | Tone: Direct financial framing, specific to Australian 3PL economics**

> Michael — following up with something a bit more specific.
>
> The 3PL leaders I respect most in Australia are running tight operations. But almost all of them, when we dig into the numbers, are carrying a margin drag they know is there but haven't been able to fix cleanly.
>
> Billing disputes and late invoicing. Manual reconciliation between transport, warehouse, and finance systems. New client onboarding that takes 10 to 14 weeks and pulls operations bandwidth every time. Each of these is individually manageable. Together, across a $150 million business, they represent somewhere between $2 million and $5 million in annual margin that is being left on the table.
>
> The 3PLs closing that gap aren't just running more efficiently — they're running a materially different business model. I've been putting together how the numbers work at your revenue level.
>
> Worth a 15-minute conversation?

---

### Touch 4 — Peer CEO Reference
**Send: Day 14 | Tone: Social proof at executive level, peer introduction offer**

> Michael — I'd like to introduce you to someone.
>
> Rod Harrison is the Managing Director of a $180 million 3PL in Queensland. He invested in a platform consolidation two years ago — TMS, WMS, and billing onto one system. He went into it primarily because he was losing enterprise RFPs to competitors who could demonstrate digital capability he couldn't match.
>
> Eighteen months in, his business has won three new enterprise contracts in the $3–6 million range, reduced his billing disputes by over 70 percent, and onboarded each of the three new clients in under three weeks. He's also had one less senior operations manager than the prior year — not from a redundancy, but because the attrition wasn't backfilled. The system absorbed the capacity.
>
> Rod is open to a direct conversation with peers at similar 3PLs. No agenda, no Ramco involvement — just an MD talking to another MD.
>
> I can make the introduction if that's useful.

---

### Touch 5 — Soft CTA / Break-Up
**Send: Day 21 | Tone: Direct Australian style, clear final offer, no pressure**

> Michael — last one from me.
>
> I've shared a few things over the past weeks — the enterprise customer capability shift, the margin drag analysis, and an offer to introduce you to Rod Harrison. If none of it landed at the right time, no problem at all.
>
> When it is the right time — whether that's before your next major contract renewal or when you're thinking about the next phase of the business — I'm happy to have a direct 15-minute conversation. No pitch, no deck. Just a frank discussion about where the 3PL market in Australia is heading and what the operators positioning themselves well are doing differently.
>
> You know where to find me.
>

---

### Touch 3 — Growth Without Cost Escalation
**Send: Day 9–10 | Tone: Business growth framing, Indonesian market specific**

> Dear Bapak Hendra,
>
> Following on from my previous message — I wanted to share one observation that is specific to 3PL businesses at your scale in Indonesia.
>
> The growth opportunity from e-commerce and manufacturing sector logistics in Indonesia is real and substantial. The risk is that every phase of growth in a manually operated 3PL requires a proportional increase in headcount and operational overhead. The business grows, but the cost curve grows with it. The margin does not improve — sometimes it shrinks — because the new revenue requires more people to deliver it than the old revenue required.
>
> The 3PL businesses in Southeast Asia that have broken this pattern have invested in operational infrastructure before they needed it — not after. They grow revenue while holding headcount flat, or growing it at a fraction of the revenue growth rate.
>
> I have a short analysis of how this works in financial terms for a 3PL at your revenue level, with conservative assumptions. Would you like me to share it?

---

### Touch 4 — Peer CEO Reference + Introduction Offer
**Send: Day 15–16 | Tone: Warm, relationship-led, peer-to-peer offer**

> Dear Bapak Hendra,
>
> I would like to share something more concrete than analysis.
>
> The CEO of a 3PL in Southeast Asia — a business of comparable scale to yours, running managed transport and contract warehousing across multiple cities — made an investment in platform consolidation approximately two years ago. His primary motivation was not technology. It was competitive: he was losing enterprise tender submissions to companies that could show a customer portal, digital documentation, and API connectivity that his operation could not yet offer.
>
> Two years on, he has won four enterprise contracts he had previously lost. His billing disputes have fallen to near zero. And he has grown his revenue by 35 percent with the same operational headcount he had before the investment.
>
> He is willing to speak directly with CEOs and owners of similar businesses — a peer conversation, with no vendor involvement. In his words: he wishes someone had made him the same offer before he made his decision.
>
> I would be glad to facilitate an introduction if it would be of value to you.

## Usage Notes

**Sender seniority is non-negotiable for this persona.**
A CEO receiving a LinkedIn message from a junior sales rep will not respond — and in some cases it damages the relationship before it starts. Every message in this sequence must come from the Managing Director, Country Head, or VP level at Ramco. If that person does not have an active LinkedIn presence, fix that before activating this sequence. A sparse LinkedIn profile for the sender is an immediate credibility signal that something is wrong.

**Before sending any sequence:**
1. Research the CEO's LinkedIn activity for the past 60 days — if they have posted about a specific business challenge, a market shift, or a new contract win, reference it in Touch 1 instead of the generic version
2. Check for mutual connections — a shared connection noted in Touch 1 ("we're both connected to [Name]") can increase acceptance rates by 40–60% for this persona
3. Check HubSpot for any prior relationship history — has this company ever been in a Ramco deal? Has any contact at this company previously replied to outreach? Know the history before sending anything
4. Confirm the CEO's correct title — President, MD, CEO, and Owner all carry different nuances in different regional contexts; get it right
5. For Indonesia and Saudi Arabia: spend 10 minutes on the company's website and LinkedIn before sending Touch 1 to find one genuinely specific reference to their business (a recent expansion, a new client win, a published article) — warmth from genuine research is detectable and dramatically improves response rates for relationship-first cultures

**Message length discipline:**
CEO messages must be shorter than CIO and COO messages. If you are editing a message and it exceeds 150 words in the body, cut it. CEOs decide in the first three lines whether to keep reading. The rest of the message justifies what the first three lines promised.

**The peer reference in Touch 4:**
This is the most important touch in the CEO sequence. A peer CEO testimonial or introduction offer outperforms any content, any analysis, and any product claim for this persona. If Ramco does not yet have a named, willing reference CEO in a given region, Touch 4 should be adapted to reference an anonymous peer with specific, credible metrics — and the offer of a peer introduction should be replaced with a direct executive briefing offer from the Ramco MD instead.

**Parallel activity running simultaneously:**
- LinkedIn paid ABM Layer 1 campaigns should be serving this CEO impressions throughout the sequence — so when Touch 2 arrives, they have likely already seen Ramco content in their feed (see `skill-07-paid-linkedin-campaigns.md`)
- If the email address is available, the CEO nurture track (Track 3 in `skill-04-email-nurture-sequences.md`) should run in parallel — not instead of this sequence
- The combination of LinkedIn organic outreach + LinkedIn paid impressions + email creates a surround effect that significantly increases the probability of a reply to Touch 3 or Touch 4

**When a CEO replies:**
- Respond within 2 hours — not 4 hours. CEO attention windows are short.
- If they express interest: do not default to a product demo. Offer the executive briefing or the peer CEO introduction first — this is what the sequence was building toward. A demo comes after the relationship is established.
- If they reply with a specific business challenge or question: answer it directly and thoroughly before offering any next step. Demonstrating that you understand their business earns the right to ask for time.
- If they refer you to a COO or CIO: accept gracefully, reach out to that contact via the relevant sequence (`linkedin-sequence-coo.md` or `linkedin-sequence-cio.md`), and keep the CEO copied or informed at key milestones — they referred you, and maintaining that connection respects the gesture

**Regional timing reminders:**
- Australia: CEOs are often hard to reach in January (summer holidays) and the first two weeks of February (catchup from the break); the window from March to November is the most productive. Check if they follow AFL — major finals week in Melbourne is a write-off.
- Indonesia: Do not send Touch 1 on a Monday — Indonesian executives often use Monday morning to manage internal priorities. Tuesday through Thursday is optimal. Avoid the two weeks surrounding Lebaran entirely — even if the CEO is working, the cultural context is not right for a new business relationship opener.
- Saudi Arabia: The work week is Sunday to Thursday. Send touches on Sunday morning or Tuesday — these are the highest-engagement days for Saudi LinkedIn users based on platform data. During Ramadan, shift sends to after 9pm AST — many Saudi executives work late after Iftar and check LinkedIn then.
- Philippines: Wednesday and Thursday mornings (8–10am PHT) are the highest-engagement windows for Philippine C-suite LinkedIn usage. Avoid the entire Holy Week period and anything after December 15 — the Filipino Christmas season is genuine and executives are mentally elsewhere.
